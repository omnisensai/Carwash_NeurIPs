\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\paragraph{From the reproducibility crisis to a cell-level control problem.}
A decade of work has treated irreproducibility as a failure to re-obtain a published
number: missing artifacts, underspecified protocols, and implementation drift
\citep{baker2016reproducibility,gundersen2018state,pineau2021improving}. In machine
learning the same agenda produced checklists, code release policies, and
kernel-level fixes for run-to-run numerical variation
\citep{pineau2021improving,he2025nondeterminism}. For language-model decision
modules a further source appears once artifacts and numerics are held fixed. Under
stochastic decoding the emitted action is a random variable
\citep{Ouyang_2025,atil2025nondeterminismdeterministicllmsettings}. An
underspecified input can yield a stable incorrect action or a mixture of actions;
where the unit of interest is the operational decision rather than a mean score,
both are failures to obtain a result.

The present challenge is therefore not only whether an experiment can be rerun, but
whether a declared input makes the decision a function of the input rather than of
the sample. Sufficiency is a property of the triple (weights, specification, task),
not of how explicit the specification looks to a reader. The shared substrate
names the object, the service location, the objective, and the reason the incorrect
action fails to achieve it. It does not name the correct action anywhere in its
text. Seventeen of twenty-three models become reproducibly correct under it. Six do
not and remain operationally uncontrolled: two emit the incorrect action on all ten
samples, two improve without reaching unanimity, one is unchanged from its baseline
state, and one moves away from the intended action.

Human-legible emphasis is not the same object. The objective-emphasis prompt states
the goal in capitals and leaves fourteen of the twenty-three models reproducibly
incorrect. The substrate states the binding relations among object, location and
movement, and leaves two. What must be made explicit is the relation that rules out
the incorrect action, not the intensity of the request.

Reproducibility engineering for a decision module is therefore the cell test after
the substrate is supplied, including on models for which the same substrate already
works elsewhere among the twenty-three. We do not claim that a denser substrate would
control the six residual cells, nor that those models cannot implement the task under
some other substrate. We claim only that this one, sufficient for seventeen models,
is not sufficient for them --- and that recovering a published metric from an
underspecified prompt is a different protocol from making a decision reproducibly
correct.


\subsection{Operational Sufficiency}
\label{sec:operational-sufficiency}

A deployed decision module either satisfies its requirement or it does not. The
requirement here is stated in advance and admits no partial credit: under a declared
condition, every execution names the intended action. Measured against that
requirement, an improvement in aggregate correctness is not evidence of anything in
particular. Expert role assignment produces the largest aggregate movement of the
seven conventional interventions, and converts one of the sixteen reproducibly
incorrect models. The other fifteen are exactly where they were. An aggregate figure
sums over models that changed state and models that did not, and reports a number
that belongs to neither.

The two conditions in which the aggregate points the wrong way make the objection
concrete rather than rhetorical. Encouragement yields fewer correct executions than
no intervention at all, 30 of 230 against 34, while the count of reproducibly
incorrect models rises from sixteen to seventeen; anti-hallucination yields more
correct executions, 41 against 34, while that count stays fixed at sixteen and one
model moves from inconsistent to consistent error. A practitioner reading either
figure alone would draw a conclusion the underlying states contradict.

DeepSeek V3.2 shows the two readings coming apart inside a single cell. At baseline
and under all seven conventional conditions it names the incorrect action on all ten
samples. Under the substrate it names the intended action on eight. Read as an
accuracy figure, that is the substrate's clearest effect on this model: nothing else
moved it at all. Read as an operational state it is a failure of the same
intervention, because the emitted action is still a function of the sample and the
eight correct runs license no claim about the ninth. The substrate worked on this
model as an evaluation intervention and did not work as a control. An accuracy-only
report cannot express that difference, which is why $8/10$ reads there as a success.

Reproducibly correct, reproducibly incorrect and non-reproducible are therefore not
three points on a scale but three operational conditions with different consequences.
The distinction that matters is not how often a model is wrong but whether being
wrong is detectable. A non-reproducible model discloses its own unreliability to
the cheapest available check: run the input twice and the answers differ. A
reproducibly incorrect model passes that check and every repetition of it. Sixteen
of the twenty-three models in the study population fail in the second way, which
means that the standard deployment-time test certifies the majority of them as
stable while they are uniformly wrong. An intervention that moves a model from the
first state to the second has made the system worse in a way no accuracy figure
will show, and five of the seven conventional interventions do exactly that.

Because the operational state depends on the input text as much as on the weights,
the unit that can carry a reliability claim is the model--condition pair rather than
the model. One of the twenty-three illustrates the range. Claude Sonnet 5, on one
question, across nine wordings, is reproducibly correct under five of them,
non-reproducible under two, and reproducibly incorrect under two. The weights,
decoding configuration and retry policy are identical throughout; only the
surrounding text differs. There is no single fact about this model's reliability on
this decision to report.

A score obtained under one wording therefore establishes performance under that
wording. It does not, by itself, establish a wording-independent capability, and
treating it as one attributes to the model a property that the measurement locates
in the pair. This is not a claim that models lack capabilities, nor that all
wordings are equivalent --- the results show they are not. It is a claim about what a
single measurement licenses: the condition under which a system will be operated is
part of what is being tested, and a reliability claim that omits it is
underdetermined in the same way the baseline task itself is.


The consequences of the distinction are not confined to how a result is reported. A
decision module of this kind sits at a branch point: its output selects a tool call,
a route, or the context passed downstream. A reproducibly incorrect module sends
every case down the same wrong branch, consistently and without signalling
anything --- the surrounding system observes a stable component and builds on it. A
non-reproducible module sends identical cases down different branches, so two runs
of the same workflow diverge at a point no component-level score records. Neither
condition is visible in an aggregate figure for the module, and neither is repaired
by a workflow that aggregates several such modules. Reliability has to be
established at the decision boundaries themselves, and reproducible correctness at
one boundary does not establish it for a chain of them.

\subsection{Specification as the Experimental Variable}
\label{sec:specification-variable}

Conventional prompt interventions and the substrate reach the model through the same
channel. Neither touches parameters, decoding or retry policy; both are text placed
around an unchanged question. What differs is what the text specifies. The seven
conventional interventions specify a disposition --- how carefully the model should
reason, who it should imagine itself to be, what it should avoid, how much the
outcome matters. The substrate specifies the task: what the object is, where it must
end up, and which movements do and do not bring it there. Through one channel, with
the question fixed, the first kind of text leaves fifteen of sixteen reproducibly
incorrect models where it found them and the second moves thirteen of them to
reproducible correctness.

Supplying that task content is the intervention under study, not leakage into the
evaluation. The two framings correspond to different experiments. Asking whether a
model recovers unstated task semantics unaided is a question about the model: it
measures what the model supplies when the input does not. Asking whether supplying
those semantics establishes reproducible correctness is a question about the input:
it measures what a specification has to contain before a decision becomes a function
of it. These are not a stricter and a weaker version of one test. A system that will
be deployed with a fixed input is described by the second; the first describes a
system operated without one.

The substrate does not contain the correct action. It names the object, the service
location, the objective, and the reason the incorrect action fails to achieve the
objective. The word naming the correct action appears nowhere in its 585 characters;
the word naming the incorrect action appears twice. Whatever moves thirteen models
from ten wrong answers to ten correct ones, it is not the presence of the answer.

The distinction the substrate encodes is between output admissibility and
interpretive correctness. Both actions are admissible outputs of the question as
posed: each is a single word naming a way to cover fifty metres, and a reader who
treats the question as being about distance will prefer the wrong one. Which action
is correct depends on relations the question leaves unstated --- that the object must
arrive, that it is not portable, that one of the two movements leaves it behind.
Where multiple outputs are admissible in principle, reliability is not a property the
model can supply on its own; it depends on the conditions under which each output is
correct having been specified.

We refer to constructing and testing such a specification against the criterion in
Equation~\eqref{eq:reproducible-correctness} as Substrate Engineering. The practice
treats the specification as an experimental artifact rather than an incidental
wrapper: it is written, hashed, versioned, and tested per model--condition, and it
succeeds or fails per model--condition. Six of the twenty-three models in this study
are not rescued by the substrate reported here, two of them not at all. That is not
a caveat attached to a general claim; it is the form the claim takes. Sufficiency is
established for particular pairs on a particular task, and neither a universal
specification nor transfer to other tasks is demonstrated by these results.


\subsection{A Behavioural Reference for Causal Analysis}
\label{sec:behavioural-reference}

Internal analysis of a language model's decision needs a behavioural object to
explain, and a single successful generation is not one. A model that emits the
intended action on four of ten samples emits it on some runs and not others; the
activations recorded during one of those runs describe that run. Treating them as
the mechanism of the model's success presumes a stable success that the sampling
does not show. The difficulty is not that such activations are uninformative but
that the thing being explained moves between executions, so a mechanistic account
of it cannot be checked by repeating the measurement.

The criterion supplies a stable object. A model--condition pair that is reproducibly
incorrect fails on all ten executions; the same model under the substrate is
reproducibly correct on all ten. Both ends of that transition are verified before
any activation is read, and the transition is a property of the pair rather than of
a run. Stage two is therefore anchored: it asks what changes internally between two
conditions whose behaviour is already established, rather than what is present
during one successful generation.

Against that reference, transplantation gives a causal result. Replacing the
residual stream at the answer position, at one layer, with the state from the
substrate run reverses the baseline preference in each of the four models whose
preference the substrate reverses, with no substrate token in the patched context.
Three controls bound it: a run patched with its own residual gives $\Delta M = 0.00$
in all 96 cells per model; a random direction of matched norm reaches
$|\Delta M|$ of at most $0.87$, $0.39$ and $2.00$ nats against measured effects of
$2.29$, $1.99$ and $14.75$; and the substrate's section headers with every
constraint line removed leave the margin at or below baseline in all three.

Four things this does not establish. Showing that a state causally affects the
measured preference is not identifying the mechanism that produces the preference;
the transplant relocates an effect without describing the computation that built
it. Showing that a state is sufficient to reverse the preference is not showing that
it is necessary, and the ablations indicate it is not: on the three reversing models
for which we measured leave-one-out effects, no single constraint line is required
--- removing any one costs at most $1.11$, $1.65$ and $2.75$ nats against margins of
$+1.51$, $+15.01$ and $+7.25$, and the reversal survives every deletion. A reversed
margin is not reproducibly correct behaviour, which is measured by sampling and not
by a forward pass. And the shallowest layer at which the transplant takes effect is
a threshold of that intervention at that position, not the layer at which the
decision is formed; a state sufficient from layer 15 may have been determined
earlier, later, or across many positions the transplant does not touch.

Internal claims here are therefore scoped to the verified behavioural transition and
to the specific intervention tested. What the measurements support is that the
substrate's effect on the decision is present as a state at the answer position, is
transferable without the text that produced it, and is not reducible to any one of
the constraint lines that produced it.
