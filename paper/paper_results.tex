\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Behavioural Results}
\label{sec:behavioural-results}

The question is whether a model that fails this decision can be moved to
reproducible correctness. We measured 23 models that fail it at baseline, either
confidently wrong or unreliable: each names the wrong action on all ten baseline
samples, or names different actions across them. Fifteen are sampled
through hosted APIs and eight are open-weight models sampled locally in bf16 with a
per-sample seed recorded. Every model receives byte-identical prompt text, $R=10$
samples per condition, giving 230 executions per condition. The baseline prompt,
the seven conventional prompts and the substrate are reproduced verbatim in
Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}, each with the sha256 digest recorded with every
execution.

\paragraph{Baseline operational state.}
Sixteen of the 23 models are reproducibly incorrect, naming the wrong action on all
ten samples. Seven are non-reproducible. Thirty-four of 230 executions name the
intended action.

\paragraph{Effects of conventional prompt interventions.}
Seven conventional interventions were applied to the same question under the same
protocol: expert role assignment, objective emphasis, chain-of-thought elicitation,
anti-hallucination instruction, threat, encouragement, and error-avoidance
instruction. Each adds a single line to the baseline prompt, inserted between the
question and the answer instruction, except expert role assignment, which precedes
the question; the texts are reproduced verbatim in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.
Table~\ref{tab:conditions} gives the operational state of the 23 models under every
condition, abbreviated RC for reproducibly correct, NR for non-reproducible and RI
for reproducibly incorrect. Expert role assignment converts one of the
sixteen reproducibly incorrect models to reproducible correctness; no conventional
condition converts more than one, and across all seven the converted model is the
same one. Four of the seven leave at least fifteen of the sixteen reproducibly
incorrect. Five of the seven move a non-reproducible model to reproducible
incorrectness: one each under anti-hallucination, chain-of-thought, error avoidance
and threat, and two under encouragement.

\paragraph{Substrate-conditioned operational state.}
Under the substrate, supplied as a system message with the question unchanged,
seventeen of the 23 models are reproducibly correct, four are non-reproducible, and
two are reproducibly incorrect (Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen3-4B-2507);
Table~\ref{tab:per-model} gives every model's count at both conditions. One hundred and
ninety-three of 230 executions name the intended action. Thirteen of the sixteen
models that are reproducibly incorrect at baseline and four of the seven that are
non-reproducible reach reproducible correctness, in a single pass with the question
unchanged and no sampling, voting or retry. No model moves to reproducible
incorrectness. One model reproducibly incorrect at baseline reaches the intended
action on most but not all samples (DeepSeek V3.2, 0/10 to 8/10); it names the
incorrect action on all ten samples at baseline and under all seven conventional
conditions. One model names the intended action less often than at baseline
(Qwen2.5-0.5B, 7/10 to 3/10) without leaving the non-reproducible state, and one is
unchanged sample for sample (Llama 3.2-3B, 4/10 at both conditions, the same four
sample indices).

\subsection{The Decision Margin}
\label{sec:internal-results}

The behavioural result counts sampled outputs. The two sections that follow
measure the same intervention inside the nine models whose weights we hold, and
establish two things the counts cannot. Section~\ref{sec:internal-results} shows
that the change in output corresponds to a large change in the underlying
decision distribution rather than to favourable sampling.
Section~\ref{sec:transplant} shows that it is produced by the state the
substrate puts the model in, and not by the disturbance of supplying some
alternative framing.

Nine of the 23 models were run locally in bf16 on the same prompt files as the
behavioural runs. The readout is the decision margin
\begin{equation}
M \;=\; \log \sum_{t \in \mathcal{D}} P(t) \;-\; \log \sum_{t \in \mathcal{W}} P(t)
\end{equation}
in nats at the \emph{answer position} --- the final token of the prompt once the
model's chat template has been applied, whose next-token distribution is the
model's first answer word. The measurement is teacher-forced: one forward pass
over the prompt, no generation, so what is measured is the distribution the
model would sample from rather than a sampled outcome. $M>0$ favours the
intended action.

Under the substrate the margin moves toward the intended action in eight of the
nine models, by a median of $5.07$ nats and by as much as $28.67$ on
Llama 3.3-70B (Table~\ref{tab:margins}). The single exception is Qwen2.5-0.5B, at
$-0.93$. Against this, the \emph{best} of the seven conventional conditions,
selected per model and so favouring them, moves the margin by a median of
$1.35$ nats. The substrate exceeds the best conventional condition in seven of
the nine, and the separation widens with model size: on the three largest it is
$28.67$ against $8.15$, $21.16$ against $5.19$, and $8.62$ against $0.62$ nats.
Both exceptions are among the three smallest models: on Qwen2.5-1.5B the best
conventional condition is marginally ahead ($+1.35$ against $+1.12$), and on
Qwen2.5-0.5B the substrate moves the margin the wrong way. On Llama 3.2-3B the
substrate is ahead by $0.14$ nats, which given the run-to-run variation we
observe on this model is not a separation we would rely on. The $0/10 \to 10/10$ transitions of
Section~\ref{sec:behavioural-results} therefore reflect a shift in the
distribution the model samples from, not a favourable draw at temperature 1.0,
and the effect is not present in models below roughly two billion parameters.

A positive margin is not the same as a correct answer. The three models in the
lower block of Table~\ref{tab:margins} favour the intended action at the answer
position while remaining non-reproducible when sampled: at $M=+0.50$ the model
prefers the intended action in roughly $62\%$ of draws, and Qwen2.5-0.5B,
Qwen2.5-1.5B and Qwen3-0.6B return $7/10$, $6/10$ and $7/10$ at baseline. They
are therefore part of the 23-model population, which excludes only models that
are reproducibly correct at baseline. What their margins show is that an
unreliable model can lean the right way and still fail to act on it, which is
the state the substrate is asked to remove.

\subsection{Activation Transplantation}
\label{sec:transplant}

A large margin shift shows that the substrate changes what the model is
disposed to answer. It does not show that the change is carried by the
substrate's own internal state rather than by the fact of receiving some
framing other than the baseline. To separate those, we transplant the state
itself.

We run the \emph{baseline} prompt and replace the residual stream at the answer
position, at a single layer, with the residual from the substrate run at the
same position and layer. No substrate token is present anywhere in the patched
forward pass. On the three models that the substrate reverses behaviourally and
that prefer the incorrect action at the answer position, this transplant alone
reverses the preference: from layer 15 of 32 on Llama 3.1-8B, 24 of 36 on
Qwen3-8B and 38 of 80 on Llama 3.3-70B. Below those layers the patched margin
stays within half a nat of its unpatched value; above them the patched run
selects the intended action. Patching a run with its own residual gives
$\Delta M = 0.0000$ at every layer of every model, so the procedure introduces
nothing by itself.

We then repeated the transplant with each of the seven conventional conditions
as the donor, at the same position and the same layers
(Figure~\ref{fig:patching}). This sweep covers the eight models that fit on one
card; Llama 3.3-70B requires more than one and has a substrate transplant only.
Across the five of those eight that prefer the incorrect action at baseline, no
conventional donor reverses the preference at any layer, with one marginal
exception: expert role on Llama 3.2-3B, which reaches $+0.10$ nats on the model
whose entire decision spans $0.55$. The donors are not inert: they move the
margin by up to $58\%$ of the substrate's own displacement. But the movement
does not carry the decision, and its direction depends on the donor --- on
Qwen3-8B five of the seven move the margin \emph{further} from the intended
action, chain-of-thought by $4.3$ nats, and on Llama 3.1-8B the threat condition
ends below the unpatched baseline. Overwriting the residual at this position
therefore does not produce a generic drift toward the intended action; what the
patched model decides depends on which state is transplanted, and among the
prompts tested only the substrate's state carries the reversal.

\paragraph{What Sections~\ref{sec:internal-results} and~\ref{sec:transplant} do not establish.}
They locate the substrate's effect and rule out one alternative explanation for
it. They do not identify a mechanism. We do not establish what the transplanted
state encodes, which part of the substrate produces it, or why a task
specification produces it where a conventional framing does not: the transplant
operates on whole prompts and so attributes the effect to the substrate as a
whole. Nor is the depth of the transition a property of the substrate. Patching
the control condition, which states the correct answer outright, reaches its
halfway point at the same layer as the substrate in four of the five models
where the comparison is defined (not plotted), so that depth is better read as
where these models settle the decision than as where the substrate acts.
Llama 3.2-3B is reported but excluded from the causal claim: its entire decision
spans $0.55$ nats, small enough that the transplant cannot be distinguished from
a perturbation large enough to tip a nearly balanced state. Finally, the nine
models measured here contain none of the six on which the control fails
behaviourally, so they cannot speak to the models where stating the answer is
insufficient.
