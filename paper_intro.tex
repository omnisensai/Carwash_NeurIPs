\subsection{From Interpretation Drift to Substrate Engineering}
\label{sec:substrate-engineering}

Prior work introduced \emph{interpretation drift} to describe the production of
incompatible operational meanings for the same task input, and proposed Substrate
Engineering as an approach to externally specifying the task requirements that
govern model decisions \citep{nguyen2026substrates}. In the classification experiment
reported there, four frontier models agreed on 30\% of cases and produced 40 distinct
output hashes under the untreated task; with an explicit semantic specification, the
same task produced complete agreement and a single output hash across 40 runs.

We extend that work from structured classification to operational action selection.
This is not a study of model capability but of input specification: the question is
not whether a model can infer the correct action from an underspecified input, but
what must be specified for a frozen model to produce the intended action reproducibly
under stochastic inference.

Here we use the widely circulated car-wash
prompt\footnote{``I need to wash my car, the car wash is only 50 metres away, should
I walk or drive there?'' \url{https://claude.ai/share/03402365-011a-475e-8ef7-9ee81ada4516}}
as a controlled test of operational decision-making. Untreated, no model is
reproducibly correct: models are either confidently wrong or alternate between
correct and incorrect across repeated executions. We then engineer cross-model
convergence, showing that a single semantic specification establishes reproducible
correctness in 14 of 17 models. The three that do not reach it define the
engineering question: what is the minimum semantic structure required for a model to
produce consistent correctness?

We further show that substrate-conditioned computations differ internally. In
open-weight models, transplanting a substrate-conditioned activation into the
untreated computation can reverse its final \texttt{DRIVE}--\texttt{WALK} preference.
